Agrimor.AI

  • Lead UI/UX and development
  • Aerodyne Geospatial
  • 2021–2026
  • Web and mobile
  • Team of 7: 3 designers, 4 DevOps engineers
  • 8 releases

Agrimor.AI is a web and mobile precision agriculture platform that uses AI to analyse crop health and brings plantation and farm data into one place: maps, production, harvest, upkeep, weighbridge and expenditure. I led its UI/UX design, prototyping and end-to-end development, managed a team of 7 (3 designers and 4 DevOps engineers) across 8 releases, and worked with the Chief Product Officer on roadmap and feature design.

Context

Plantation teams track upkeep, harvest, weighbridge and spending across many blocks. Agrimor.AI puts all of that on one map-based platform for estate managers, supervisors and field staff, so they can see what is happening in each block and act on it.

Analysing crop health

Agrimor.AI turns data from drones, sensors and smart devices into a picture of how crops are doing, block by block. The AI analysis covers plant health, tree stress and vigour, chlorophyll, water uptake, tree count and crown area, crop coverage and vacant points, soil content and erosion, and slope and elevation. Teams use the predicted trends to make better decisions about water, labour and nutrients.

The same platform runs daily operations: harvest, upkeep, task tracking, weighbridge, attendance, logistics and expenditure. My job was to make all of it readable on one map, so a manager can move from a health problem to the task that fixes it.

Designing around the map

The map is the main surface. Estates and blocks sit in a tree on the left, analytics on the right, and a month timeline underneath lets people step through time without leaving the map.

A dark theme for the control room

The dark interface uses a lime accent on charcoal so status and totals stand out on the map, including on a wall display.

On the phone

The mobile app extends the platform to the field: switch irrigation zones, post crop issues to a community feed and ask questions of farm data.

I kept the mobile app to what people need in the field: irrigation switches, a feed for crop issues and an assistant for questions about farm data. Heavier analytics stay on the web version.

More web screens

The same system carries across products: crop management and estate analytics on the web, and a pond monitor.

Flows and logic

Before screens, I mapped the journeys and the system logic behind them.

A shared design system

I built a scalable UI component library and design system, now used across 3 products, so they look and behave consistently.

Outcome

  • Redesigning the core user journeys cut task completion time from 3 hours to 15 minutes.
  • Adoption grew 40% across 15+ enterprise accounts.
  • Client operational efficiency rose 35%, measured by tasks completed per user per week.

GeoEyez

  • UI/UX design
  • Web dashboard
  • Pipeline monitoring

GeoEyez helps teams monitor the ground along a pipeline. It shows where signs of distress, such as erosion, leaks and water ponding, appear by kilometre point, and what the AI detected at each site.

Context

Pipeline teams need to find and rank ground problems along a long corridor. GeoEyez gives them a map, a severity score by kilometre point and an AI-assisted view of each site.

From wireframe to final

I started with low-fidelity layouts for a site severity score and a site analyzer, then reworked them into the dashboard below.

The dashboard

One screen answers three questions: where is the problem (map), how bad is it (severity by KP) and what kind is it (sign of distress statistics).

Data shown is sample data.

Decisions and outcome

Severity by KP comes first because it tells teams which sites need attention. The detail screen then lets them pick a survey date and compare what the AI detected, so they can judge whether a site is getting better or worse.
